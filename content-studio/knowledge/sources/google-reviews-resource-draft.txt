# How to Get More Google Reviews: A Practical Guide for Local Businesses

Google reviews are not decoration on your Business Profile. They influence how customers evaluate your business, whether they trust you, and sometimes whether they ever click through to your website. For local businesses, that makes review acquisition an operating responsibility, not an occasional marketing campaign.

The good news is that getting more Google reviews does not require buying reviews, pestering customers, or installing complicated software. It requires three things: a direct path to your review page, a consistent invitation to real customers, and a process that keeps working after the initial push.

## Do Google reviews help SEO?

Yes. [Google confirms](https://support.google.com/business/answer/7091?hl=en) that more reviews and positive ratings can help a business's local ranking. Reviews are part of a broader local search system built around relevance, distance, and prominence. A large review count cannot fix an irrelevant listing, poor business information, or an address far from the searcher.

Reviews also influence the decision after your business appears. A complete profile with recent feedback and thoughtful responses builds more confidence than an unattended listing.

The objective is not to manufacture a perfect score. It is to make real customer experiences visible, consistently, over time.

## 1. Get your Google Business Profile ready first

Before requesting a single review, confirm that your Google Business Profile is accurate and accessible. Check your business name, primary category, address or service area, phone number, hours, website, and service descriptions. Upload current photos that reflect what customers will actually encounter.

Make sure the profile is claimed and verified, with the correct people assigned access. If you operate several locations, each eligible location needs its own accurate profile and review destination.

## 2. Create your Google review link and QR code

The first operational fix is removing unnecessary steps. Do not tell customers to search for your company, select the right location, find the review section, and figure out where to click. Give them a direct Google review link.

Google's [review-link instructions](https://support.google.com/business/answer/16816815?hl=en) are straightforward:

1. Open your Business Profile on a computer.
2. Select **Read reviews**, then **Get more reviews**.
3. Copy the review link or download the Google review QR code.
4. Test both using a phone that is not logged into your business account.

Google currently provides its downloadable review QR code through a desktop browser.

For businesses managing multiple locations, verify every destination before printing. A customer standing in your Tampa location should not be sent to review your Orlando location.

## 3. Ask every eligible customer, not just the happy ones

Most businesses underperform at collecting reviews because asking is inconsistent. One employee remembers; another forgets.

Build a neutral invitation into a natural post-service touchpoint. After checkout, delivery, a completed appointment, or a finished project, let customers know they can share an honest review when convenient. Keep the language simple:

**“Thank you for choosing us. If you'd like to share your experience, you can leave an honest Google review using this link.”**

Do not sort customers by satisfaction before sending the request. Do not send satisfied customers to Google while directing unhappy customers into a private form. That is review gating, and it conflicts with Google's rules against selectively soliciting positive reviews.

You may invite feedback. You may not control what customers say, require a rating, or pressure them to post while standing in front of staff.

## 4. Put review requests where customers already interact

A review link hidden in a footer rarely becomes a reliable acquisition channel. Place an optional invitation in the customer journey where it is useful and expected.

Good touchpoints include receipts, post-purchase emails, service completion messages, pickup bags, take-home instructions, and countertop displays. Match the material to the service: an invoice, aftercare card, or service summary.

A **Google review QR code** works particularly well on printed materials because the customer can open the link with a phone camera. The design should explain what happens after the scan. Use a clear call to action such as **“Share your experience on Google.”** Avoid language suggesting a five-star rating is expected.

## 7. Follow up after the transaction without creating spam

Pair physical reminders with timely, permission-based digital requests after the service is complete.

**Email example:** “Thanks for choosing [Business]. Your feedback helps others make an informed decision. If you'd like to share your experience, here's our Google review link: [link].”

**Text example:** “Thanks for visiting [Business]. You're welcome to share an honest Google review here: [link].”

Send requests to the full eligible customer group under a consistent rule. Respect marketing consent, opt-outs, and applicable communication laws. Avoid repeated messages when someone has not responded. No customer owes you a review.

For healthcare and other sensitive services, use extra care with patient privacy. Never publicly confirm a patient relationship or reveal protected details in a review response.

## 8. Respond to reviews like they are part of customer service

Google recommends responding to reviews. A thoughtful response demonstrates accountability to the reviewer and to everyone else evaluating your business.

Thank customers for specific feedback without writing the same generic paragraph every time. When a review raises a concern, acknowledge it professionally and offer an appropriate private path to resolve it. Do not argue, disclose personal information, or promise a reward for changing a rating.

Not every critical review violates policy. Flag reviews only when there is a legitimate policy issue, such as spam or prohibited content. An uncomfortable but genuine review is still customer feedback.

Look for patterns involving service quality, response times, communication, and team handoffs.

## 9. Measure reviews, not just QR code scans

A scan is a useful interaction metric. It is not proof that someone submitted a review. Review submissions occur on Google's platform, where your business generally cannot attribute an individual published review to a particular printed card.

Track your starting review count and average rating, new reviews each month, responses, card distribution, and scans when available. Compare performance across locations and look at local visibility using a consistent set of searches or a geographic rank grid.

For planning, consider a hypothetical business handing out 200 cards in a month. If 50 people scan and 10 independently decide to review, that is 10 new reviews. Those figures illustrate the funnel, not a promised result.

The better question is not “How many cards did we buy?” It is “Are we creating more genuine opportunities for customers to share their experiences?”

## 10. Keep your review program within Google's rules

[Google prohibits](https://support.google.com/contributionpolicy/answer/7400114?hl=en) fake engagement, paid reviews, review gating, and incentives for reviews, including discounts or free products. It also prohibits pressuring customers to review on-site, requiring specific review language, and directing staff to solicit a set number of reviews.

The [FTC separately regulates](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/consumer-reviews-testimonials-rule-questions-answers) deceptive review and testimonial practices, including fake reviews and paying for reviews with a required positive or negative sentiment. Google's rules are stricter about incentives generally, so do not assume a practice permitted under one standard is acceptable on Google.

Train staff to offer the opportunity, not chase a rating. Do not write reviews for customers, supply prewritten praise, or use employees and relatives to inflate your profile. A reputation built on authentic feedback is a durable asset. A manipulated one is a liability.

## Your first 30 days: build the system once

**Week 1: Establish the baseline.** Audit your Business Profile, record review count and rating, and create a tested direct review link for each location.

**Week 2: Add access points.** Place QR codes, configure follow-up messages, and train staff on neutral invitations without quotas.

**Week 3: Make it operational.** Introduce take-home cards or a tap-and-scan stand. Test destinations and avoid on-site pressure.

**Week 4: Review the data.** Compare new reviews with your baseline, respond, and adjust card distribution.

After 30 days, the objective is a repeatable process that does not depend on the owner remembering to ask.

## Frequently asked questions

### How many Google reviews does my business need?

There is no universal target. Compare relevant businesses in your actual service area, review the quality and consistency of feedback, and prioritize a sustainable invitation process. More reviews can support local rankings, but no review count guarantees a map-pack position.

### Can I offer a discount for a Google review?

No. Google prohibits incentives in exchange for reviews, regardless of whether the requested rating is positive or negative. Keep rewards, discounts, and giveaways separate from review solicitation.

### Do customers need a Gmail address to leave a Google review?

No. They need to be signed into a Google Account, but that account can be created using a non-Gmail email address.

### Are QR codes or NFC cards better for getting Google reviews?

Neither guarantees more reviews. QR codes work with phone cameras; NFC cards remove the camera step on compatible devices. Combining both provides flexibility, but the customer experience matters more than the hardware.
